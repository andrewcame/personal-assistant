# Our Ways of Working Principles

A strong engineering team doesn't require everyone to think or work in the same way. Different perspectives and healthy disagreement make us stronger, but we need a **shared understanding of how we work together**.

These principles set the **baseline behaviours expected of everyone in the team**. They provide guardrails for how we communicate, collaborate, make decisions and take collective responsibility. 

They help us to:

- challenge ideas constructively and respectfully;
- communicate openly and share context;
- clarify ownership and decision-making;
- collaborate effectively and surface issues early; and
- take shared responsibility for delivery and quality.

They are **guardrails, not a rulebook**. They won't prescribe how to handle every situation and don't replace judgement, conversation or empathy. They should guide our own behaviour and support constructive conversations when something isn't working, rather than being used to score points or win disagreements.

**Different opinions, personalities and working styles are welcome. How we work individually may differ; how we treat each other and work together is a shared expectation.**

**Principles describe the behaviours we expect. Our working practices describe how we apply them day to day and may evolve as the team learns.**


###  How we treat each other

- 
    - **Seek to understand before making assumptions**  
We ask questions, understand context and consider other perspectives before drawing conclusions about someone's actions or motivations.

- Ask for context before assuming why somebody has taken a particular approach.
- Check your understanding before responding to something you disagree with.
- Be curious about perspectives that differ from your own.
- Distinguish between what you know and what you are assuming.
- Give colleagues the opportunity to explain their reasoning.
- Avoid attributing intent where the observable behaviour is what matters.

- 
    - **Challenge ideas, respect people**  
We encourage healthy disagreement and challenge ideas directly, while treating each other with respect. We critique the idea, not the individual.

- Ask questions and seek to understand before dismissing an approach.
- Explain the reasoning behind concerns or alternative suggestions.
- Challenge assumptions, decisions and technical approaches without making the disagreement personal.
- Give others space to explain their perspective without interrupting or talking over them.
- Be willing to change your view when presented with better evidence.
- Avoid sarcasm, belittling, point-scoring or dismissive language.
- Everyone should be able to question an idea or raise a concern regardless of role or seniority. Seniority brings responsibility for creating space for challenge, not exemption from it.

- **Give and receive feedback constructively**  
We give feedback directly, respectfully and with the intention of improving outcomes. We listen to feedback with curiosity before responding or defending.

- Give feedback close to the event rather than allowing frustration to build.
- Focus on observable behaviour and its impact rather than personality or intent.
- Be specific about what would help improve the situation.
- Give sensitive feedback privately where appropriate.
- Listen to feedback before explaining or defending your position.
- Ask questions if feedback is unclear.
- Treat constructive feedback as part of healthy team working, not as a personal attack.
- Positive intent doesn't remove the need to understand and address the impact of our behaviour.
- Where concerns are repeated, significant, or difficult to resolve directly, they should be raised with your line manager.

###  How we work together

- **Participate and collaborate**

  We actively contribute to the team's planning, refinement, estimation, delivery and continuous improvement. We share our knowledge, ask questions, raise concerns and help others succeed. Participation means being engaged and contributing appropriately, not simply being present or speaking for the sake of it. We create an environment where people can ask questions, admit when they don't know something, ask for help, raise concerns and acknowledge mistakes without fear of embarrassment or blame.

- Come prepared and actively contribute to relevant team meetings, including refinement, estimation and continuous improvement.
- Ask questions, share knowledge and raise concerns when requirements, assumptions or approaches are unclear.
- Help identify risks, dependencies, unknowns and opportunities to simplify.
- Stay engaged with the team's work, not just the work you individually own.
- Be comfortable saying **“I don't know”**, asking for help and acknowledging mistakes; respond constructively when others do the same.
- Raise concerns and offer alternative perspectives regardless of role, seniority or prevailing opinion.
- Recognise that meaningful participation can take different forms and does not require everyone to contribute in the same way.

- **Work in the open**  
We use shared channels by default for team-relevant work so that knowledge, context and discussions are accessible to the team. Private conversations are appropriate where needed, but relevant context and decisions are brought back into shared spaces.

- Prefer shared team channels for discussions that may be useful to others.
- Use direct messages for genuinely private, sensitive or low-value conversations rather than as the default place for team decisions.
- Bring relevant context from private or verbal conversations back into shared spaces.
- Share useful information rather than relying on individuals to know who to ask.
- Move complicated or increasingly tense written conversations into a live conversation when that is more effective.
- Capture the outcome afterwards if it affects the wider team or work.

- **Decisions live with the work**  
We record significant decisions and changes where others can find them. Scope changes, acceptance-criteria changes, follow-up work and important implementation decisions should be captured on the relevant ticket. Longer-lived technical decisions may belong in documentation or ADRs. [Software Engineering - Principles](https://puregym.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/ENG/pages/5165744129/Software+Engineering+-+Principles#%F0%9F%93%9A-Write-It-Down%2C-Share-It-Well)

- Update the ticket when agreed scope or acceptance criteria change.
- Record significant decisions that affect what is being delivered or how it is being delivered.
- Create and link follow-up tickets when additional work is split out.
- Record when something is explicitly agreed to be out of scope.
- Capture relevant outcomes from conversations or meetings on the ticket.
- Link to ADRs or longer-lived documentation where appropriate.
- Keep enough context with the work that somebody who was not part of the original conversation can understand what changed and why.
- Focus on meaningful decisions rather than documenting every conversation.

- **Clear ownership, shared outcomes**  
Individuals can own responsibilities and decisions, but we succeed as a team. Ownership means accountability, not territory, and we help each other achieve the team's goals. We raise blockers, risks and concerns early rather than allowing them to become surprises.

- Be clear about who owns a piece of work or decision.
- Take responsibility for progressing the work you own.
- Ask for help before a blocker becomes a delivery problem.
- Offer help when your knowledge or capacity can unblock others.
- Raise risks, dependencies and concerns early.
- Look beyond your own ticket or task to the team's overall outcome.
- Avoid treating components, services or areas of code as personal territory.
- Support others working in areas where you have greater knowledge or experience.

###  How we make decisions and deliver together

- **Debate, decide, commit**  
We encourage healthy challenge before decisions are made and use evidence where possible. Not every decision requires consensus. Where we cannot reach agreement, we escalate to the appropriate decision owner rather than allowing a stalemate to persist. Once a decision is made, we commit to giving it a fair opportunity to succeed, even when it wasn't our preferred approach.

- Create space for relevant perspectives before significant decisions are made.
- Challenge assumptions and proposals constructively.
- Use evidence, data, prototypes or experiments where they can help resolve uncertainty.
- Be clear about who owns the decision and escalate when the team reaches a genuine stalemate.
- Avoid extending debate indefinitely in pursuit of unanimous agreement.
- Once a decision is made, accept it professionally and commit to making the chosen approach successful.
- Approach decisions with a give-it-a-go mindset, rather than waiting for an alternative approach to fail.
- If the approach succeeds, recognise that there can be multiple valid ways to solve a problem and be open to changing your perspective.
- If the approach doesn't succeed, focus on what we can learn and how we improve rather than assigning blame, saying “I told you so”, or revisiting who originally argued for which approach.
- Where new evidence or changed circumstances materially challenge a decision, raise them with the **decision owner**, who is responsible for determining whether the decision should be revisited. A decision should not be repeatedly reopened simply because it wasn't someone's preferred outcome.

- **Quality is everyone's responsibility**  
Quality, security, maintainability and operability are responsibilities of the whole team. Roles such as QA, Tech Lead or Platform may provide specialist expertise and hold specific accountabilities, but they do not exclusively own the outcome. Shared responsibility does not remove the responsibilities of individual roles. We treat mistakes, defects and incidents as opportunities to learn and improve the system rather than opportunities to assign blame.

- Consider quality and testing throughout delivery rather than at the end.
- Involve QA and other specialists early where their expertise can improve the outcome.
- Write and review code with maintainability, security and operability in mind.
- Raise quality concerns regardless of role or seniority.
- Take responsibility for defects or production issues as a team rather than assigning blame.
- Leave code, documentation and systems in a better state where reasonably possible.
- Recognise specialist accountabilities while avoiding language such as “that's QA's problem” or “that's Platform's responsibility”.
- Consider whether what we build can be supported and operated effectively after release.
- Be open about mistakes and problems so they can be addressed early.
- Focus first on understanding what happened and improving the system.
- Share learning from failures so others can benefit.
