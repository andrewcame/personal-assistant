# Competency: Technical

## Scale

5 levels: Learn, Execute, Mentor, Manage, Strategise

### Score 1 - Learn
- Description: Builds reliable foundations in clean, testable code and well-structured automated tests, at a pace matched to guided, well-scoped work. Follows established patterns, understands the local area they are changing, and connects that work to why it matters for the member experience rather than treating it as an abstract exercise. Uses documentation, examples, and support from others to close gaps in understanding rather than guessing, and builds speed through repetition rather than shortcuts.
- Expectation: Writes simple software or test code with guidance, using basic tooling (VCS, tests, CI) and asking why the work matters.
- Expected example: A software engineer completes small bug fixes or features with tests and clear review notes, and can explain what the fix means for the member journey it touches. A QA engineer writes simple automated tests under guidance that catch real defects before they reach production, and can explain why that check matters. Both act on review feedback quickly, asking questions to understand the reasoning rather than just making the change.
- Stretch example: Owns tricky but bounded defects to safe completion with a regression test that prevents it recurring for members. Builds early automation or test coverage that becomes the pattern a squad reuses on similar work, directly cutting rework later in the sprint. Becomes a trusted point of context in one small part of the codebase or test suite faster than guided pace alone would predict.

### Score 2 - Execute
- Description: Independently delivers well-defined work to production at a pace the squad can rely on: a software engineer ships features end-to-end, a QA engineer delivers reliable test coverage across web, app, or API surfaces, both without needing direction on what to do next. Both understand how their work connects to member-facing outcomes and PureGym's conversion, retention, and acquisition goals, not just the ticket in front of them, and both hold their pace without letting quality slip.
- Expectation: Delivers maintainable software or test code on well-defined tasks independently; gives useful reviews and understands system context.
- Expected example: A software engineer owns well-scoped stories from pickup through deployment and verification, and gives reviews that catch logic or testability issues before they reach members. A QA engineer plans, automates, and executes coverage for an initiative independently, and contributes to early acceptance-criteria conversations so the squad does not discover gaps mid-sprint. Both treat done as deployed and verified, not code-complete, and pull work to completion rather than starting something new.
- Stretch example: Shapes moderately complex features or test approaches into safe, incremental slices the squad can actually ship inside its delivery window. Raises testability or design concerns early enough to change the technical approach before build starts, saving the squad a costly rework cycle later. Helps less experienced colleagues improve through pairing, cutting their time to independent delivery.

### Score 3 - Mentor
- Description: Owns complex features or test strategy end-to-end and contributes strongly to design, working at a pace and depth that lets less experienced colleagues build faster around them. Sets a quality bar through reviews and reusable patterns, and holds a clear view of how their area's reliability and speed affect member trust and delivery confidence. Could reasonably be the squad's subject-matter expert in a tool, technology, or test discipline.
- Expectation: Sets a quality bar for software and test code, designs components and automated tests, mentors others, and improves reliability and speed.
- Expected example: A software engineer owns a complex feature or refactor end-to-end, including rollout and follow-up fixes, and creates a reusable pattern that measurably speeds up similar work across the squad. A QA engineer produces test and automation reviews that are thorough and educational, and surfaces test debt early enough that the squad fixes it before it slows delivery. Both mentor a less experienced colleague through repeated pairing rather than one-off correction, visibly raising that person's pace and confidence.
- Stretch example: Leads the technical or quality approach for an initiative slice and keeps execution coherent from Discovery through hypercare. Makes technical or test debt visible early enough that the squad changes its plan around it rather than absorbing a production incident later. Influences how work is shaped before it reaches implementation, so the squad accepts fewer initiatives it cannot deliver within the agreed window.

### Score 4 - Manage
- Description: Shapes technical or quality direction for a squad or platform, setting the standard for design quality, test architecture, and engineering discipline that others reach toward. Coaches senior colleagues, holds DORA and quality signals as first-class metrics, and makes the trade-off between pace and technical debt explicit rather than letting delivery pressure erode standards quietly.
- Expectation: Leads technical direction for a team or domain; drives architecture and test strategy, manages tech debt, and aligns across teams.
- Expected example: Sets clear technical direction for an initiative, aligns with peers before initiative acceptance so the squad hears one coherent view, and keeps delivery moving without the acceptance session turning into a live debate. Shapes test architecture and automation direction for a platform, ensuring coverage strategy is applied consistently across every squad the platform serves. Holds change failure rate and mean time to restore as real signals of health, not afterthoughts, and leads the response when either deteriorates.
- Stretch example: Creates a standard, pattern, or automation capability that other squads or platforms later adopt, lifting throughput beyond their own team. Turns around a failing technical or quality approach across several initiatives, restoring the squad's confidence to commit to what it can actually deliver. Becomes a key partner in shaping wider architecture or quality strategy, visible in how consistently other teams start asking for their input.

### Score 5 - Strategise
- Description: Defines technical or quality direction at the highest individual-contributor level, partnering closely with the Principal Software Engineer or QA and Test Engineering Manager to connect squad-level decisions to function-wide standards. Solves the hardest design or test-architecture problems, builds reusable platforms or frameworks from real delivery needs, and develops other technical leaders so the bar keeps rising without losing contact with what members actually experience.
- Expectation: Defines org-wide technical strategy, builds platforms or frameworks, anticipates trends, and measures business impact.
- Expected example: Builds a shared component or internal tool that measurably raises the pace of every engineer who consumes it, and keeps squad architecture aligned with the function's longer-term direction. Builds shared test utilities or automation infrastructure that reduce duplication across platforms and raise confidence in every release that depends on them. Develops technical and quality leaders across squads so the ceiling keeps rising, not just their own output.
- Stretch example: Partners on a reusable capability, architectural intervention, or test infrastructure investment that several squads later adopt, with measurable impact on delivery pace or defect escape rate. Solves a high-cost technical or quality problem in a way that changes wider engineering or QA practice, without stepping outside their own platform's ownership. Builds external credibility, through chapters, conferences, or communities, that reinforces the standard the whole function holds itself to.
