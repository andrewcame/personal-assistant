# Competency: Collaboration

## Scale

5 levels: Learn, Execute, Mentor, Manage, Strategise

### Score 1 - Learn
- Description: Communicates clearly about progress, blockers, and questions from day one, in a squad where engineers, QA, product, and delivery operate as one unit. Asks clarifying questions early rather than guessing, and starts to learn who needs to be brought in and when, so small misunderstandings do not turn into rework that slows the whole squad down.
- Expectation: Communicates status, seeks help early, and participates constructively in team rituals.
- Expected example: A software engineer gives clear stand-up updates on what they are doing and where they are stuck, and asks a clarifying question in refinement rather than building against an assumption. A QA engineer flags an unclear acceptance criterion to the squad before writing tests against it, saving a rework cycle later in the dev lifecycle. Both act on feedback promptly and confirm understanding rather than nodding along.
- Stretch example: Brings QA or Product into a conversation early enough to clarify a story before work drifts off scope. Shares a lesson learned from a task in a way that helps a teammate avoid repeating the same mistake, saving the squad real time. Helps a new joiner to the team understand a small area of context without being asked.

### Score 2 - Execute
- Description: Coordinates shared work across engineering and QA effectively, making decisions, assumptions, and risks visible to the right people early enough to act on them. Builds trust by being reliable and easy to work with, and brings others in, QA, Product, or peers, before ambiguity turns into wasted effort.
- Expectation: Proactively aligns with peers and stakeholders, shares context, and gives and receives actionable feedback.
- Expected example: A software engineer coordinates a change touching several components without confusion over who does what, and explains a technical decision clearly enough that teammates act on it without repeated follow-up. A QA engineer partners with engineers and Product early on edge cases, turning a vague acceptance criterion into something testable before the squad commits. Both give feedback that is specific enough to act on, not just polite.
- Stretch example: Brings together the right people to untangle a messy cross-squad issue and keeps the conversation focused until there is real clarity. Spots when a teammate is blocked by missing context and provides it before the squad loses a day to it. Starts breaking down the local silo between engineering work and QA work by sharing context that makes both faster.

### Score 3 - Mentor
- Description: Improves the quality of collaboration around the work, not just their own part of it. Adapts communication to the audience, shares context so others become more self-sufficient, and helps healthier collaborative habits, QA and engineering treating quality as shared, not handed over, become normal rather than accidental.
- Expectation: Facilitates cross-team collaboration, mentors on communication, and resolves conflicts to keep delivery moving.
- Expected example: A software engineer facilitates a feature discussion so the squad leaves with clearer alignment, not just more opinions, and the initiative moves faster as a result. A QA engineer mentors colleagues on test design so quality becomes a shared discipline rather than a QA handover, visibly reducing late-stage defects. Both handle a difficult piece of feedback constructively and keep the conversation productive rather than letting it stall delivery.
- Stretch example: Resolves a recurring friction point between engineering and QA and leaves behind a healthier working pattern that outlasts the specific issue. Builds enough trust with stakeholders, Product, Finance, or Commercial, that they actively seek this person's input before a decision is made. Creates a regular knowledge-sharing rhythm that measurably reduces confusion or rework across the squad.

### Score 4 - Manage
- Description: Strengthens collaboration as a system across a squad or platform. Spots gaps in handoffs and alignment between engineering, QA, product, and delivery, and coaches others to close them, so the whole group stays coordinated even when delivery pressure is high and the pace is fast.
- Expectation: Drives cross-team outcomes and chapter participation, improving collaboration systems and information flow across teams.
- Expected example: Improves how initiative acceptance runs so the squad leaves with clear ownership and fewer kickbacks mid-sprint. Fosters shared quality ownership across a platform, so testability and acceptance criteria are treated as everyone's job rather than a QA gate at the end. Coaches others through stakeholder or alignment issues rather than stepping in and doing it for them every time.
- Stretch example: Redesigns a collaboration pattern across squads or platforms that measurably reduces friction, rework, or cycle time. Steps into a sensitive disagreement between teams, restores trust, and leaves a practical way forward that holds under pressure. Becomes the trusted bridge stakeholders reach for when a cross-team initiative needs clean coordination to hit its delivery window.

### Score 5 - Strategise
- Description: Shapes how the squad, platform, and wider engineering function align with each other and with the business. Uses chapters and partnership with the Principal Software Engineer or QA and Test Engineering Manager to turn local patterns into function-wide ones, translating complex technical or quality trade-offs into language that lets Finance, Commercial, or Product make confident decisions.
- Expectation: Shapes org-wide collaboration through chapters and shared goals, aligns teams to business outcomes, and influences senior stakeholders.
- Expected example: Builds communication patterns that keep the squad aligned with dependent teams without adding noise, translating a complex architecture trade-off into something a non-technical stakeholder can act on. Builds trusted relationships with Finance or Commercial stakeholders on payment or conversion-critical coverage, giving them the confidence to sign off a launch on real data rather than a gut feel. Strengthens chapter relationships so useful patterns move faster around the function.
- Stretch example: Resolves a high-stakes alignment issue spanning the squad and senior stakeholders, and leaves behind a stronger working pattern than existed before. Partners on a collaboration or reporting mechanism that several squads or platforms later adopt. Becomes a trusted voice who can align difficult trade-offs with the business while staying grounded in what the squad can actually ship.
